## Slide 1 — Title (0:00–1:00)

[SLIDE: Title, presenter name, date, "Internal training — not client-facing."]

**Good morning, everyone. Thank you for making the time. Today we're going to talk about something that sounds simple and is actually the hardest part of a creative agency: finding the right people, deciding quickly and fairly, and keeping them.**

**My goal for the next forty-five minutes is not to give you a motivational talk. It's to give you a system you can run on a normal Tuesday when three projects are late and two candidates are waiting for an answer.**

**By the end, you'll know the five decisions you are never allowed to make alone, the six stages every creative goes through, and the exact queue you open every morning.**

---

## Slide 2 — Why this matters (1:00–5:00)

[SLIDE: Three stat cards — "Silence loses good people", "Memory is not a roster", "Speed is a promise".]

**Let me start with a uncomfortable truth. Most creative agencies don't lose great talent because they reject them. They lose them because they go quiet.**

**A photographer sends a portfolio. Nobody replies for eleven days. By day four, she's accepted another shoot. By day eleven, our email is just proof that we were never serious.**

**The second truth: our roster lives in someone's head. We know who's good with natural light, who can handle a difficult client, who's available in two weeks. But if that person is on vacation, the knowledge is gone. That's not a roster. That's a rumor.**

**The third truth: speed is a promise we make with every project. When we staff slowly, the client feels it. When we staff badly, the client sees it.**

**So the system we're building has one job: make the right decision visible, fast, and on the record.**

---

## Slide 3 — The four verticals (5:00–8:00)

[SLIDE: Four columns — Photography, Videography, Content & Social, Design & Creative — with 3–4 role examples each.]

**We hire across four verticals, and each one has a different definition of good.**

**Photography is about light, consistency, and retouching. A great event photographer and a great editorial photographer are not interchangeable.**

**Videography is about story, sound, and edit. A brilliant DP can be a mediocre editor. We have to stop treating "video person" as one job.**

**Content and Social is about platform fluency, copy, cadence, and brand safety. It's the closest thing we have to a daily relationship with the client's audience.**

**Design and Creative is about concept, craft, systems, and file hygiene. A beautiful deck with broken source files is a liability.**

**Same agency, four different rubrics. That's why the evaluation framework is not one checklist. It's four.**

---

## Slide 4 — The current state (8:00–12:00)

[SLIDE: A messy flow: DM → spreadsheet → email → memory → contract → panic.]

**Here's how it works today, and I'm going to be blunt because we're all adults.**

**Someone sees a portfolio on Instagram. They send it to someone else. It sits in a direct message. Eventually an admin creates a contractor record with a name, a business name, a role, and a state. That's it. That's the profile.**

**There is no application. There is no score. There is no record of why we said yes or no. There is no availability. When a project lands, we ask around. "Who's free?" is our capacity planning system.**

**And when the contract is ready, we assemble it from a template. That part actually works well. The platform is good at the back half. The front half is a group chat.**

**We are not going to replace the back half. We are going to put a real front door on it.**

---

## Slide 5 — The principle: human decides, system remembers (12:00–16:00)

[SLIDE: Two columns — "System does" / "Human decides".]

**Here is the design principle, and if you remember nothing else, remember this: the system prepares, the human decides, the system remembers.**

**The system can acknowledge, deduplicate, score against a rubric, schedule, remind, assemble a draft, assign training, route a review, and flag a late deliverable.**

**The human decides whether to hire. The human sets the rate. The human signs the contract. The human gives final approval. The human shares private data. The human validates a lead.**

**Why draw the line there? Three reasons. Legal: a promise made by an assistant is still a promise made by the agency. Fairness: a score is not a decision, and a rubric can encode bias if no one owns it. Trust: talent and clients need a name, not a bot.**

**So when I say "automation," I do not mean "autopilot." I mean "the paperwork moves without a person chasing it."**

---

## Slide 6 — The five hard stops (16:00–20:00)

[SLIDE: Five red boxes — Employment promises, Compensation/contracts, Hiring decisions, Private data, Unverified leads.]

**These are the five hard stops. No automation, no assistant, no AI model may cross them. I want to say them slowly.**

**One: employment promises. Never tell someone they have the job, the hours, or the volume until a human has approved it.**

**Two: compensation and contracts. Never quote a rate. Never send a contract. Never imply terms.**

**Three: hiring decisions. Never advance, reject, or "soft yes" a candidate on your own authority.**

**Four: private data. Tax forms, IDs, bank details, client-confidential briefs. They do not go in chat, email, or a model prompt.**

**Five: unverified leads. If a project is not confirmed in the system, it is not a project. Do not staff against a maybe.**

**If you're ever unsure, the answer is escalate. Escalation is not a delay. Silence is the delay.**

---

## Slide 7 — The journey (20:00–25:00)

[SLIDE: Horizontal journey with 10 stages and a clock under each.]

**Here's the journey we're going to run. Ten stages, each with an owner and a clock.**

**Apply. Acknowledge. Triage. Review. Interview. Verify. Decide. Contract. Onboard. Deliver.**

**The promises are simple. Acknowledgment within one business day. First human review within two. A decision within seven business days of a complete application. Contract assembly within two business days of an approved SOW. Readiness within ten business days of a signed contract. Review of a submitted deliverable within two business days.**

**Those numbers are the product. If we miss them, the system tells us before the candidate or the client does.**

---

## Slide 8 — Stage one: application (25:00–29:00)

[SLIDE: Application form wireframe with fields grouped: About you, Your work, Your availability, Consent.]

**The application has four groups. About you: name, contact, city, timezone, role. Your work: portfolio link, reel link, up to ten samples. Your availability: start date, weekly capacity, rate expectation, engagement preference. Consent: process my data, keep me on file for twelve months.**

**That's it. No twenty-field form. Every field must earn its place by changing a decision.**

**On submit, three things happen automatically. We store the record. We send an acknowledgment with a reference number and a date. We create a queue item with a two-day clock.**

**And notice what we do not do: we do not promise anything. The acknowledgment says "we will review and respond by this date." That's a promise we can keep.**

---

## Slide 9 — Stage two: triage (29:00–32:00)

[SLIDE: Decision tree — duplicate? eligible? complete? link works?]

**Triage is where we protect the candidate from our own chaos.**

**First, duplicates. Same email, same person, two applications. We link them. We never overwrite the first record, because the history matters.**

**Second, eligibility. Is the role in scope? Is the country eligible? Is consent present? Is the portfolio reachable?**

**Third, completeness. If something is missing, we don't reject. We ask for the specific item and we pause the clock. There's a big difference between "not good enough" and "we couldn't open your link."**

**A hold is not a no. A hold is a task.**

---

## Slide 10 — Stage three: portfolio review (32:00–37:00)

[SLIDE: Rubric table — six criteria, 1–5 scale, weight, evidence note.]

**Every vertical has six criteria. Each is scored one to five. Each score needs one line of evidence.**

**For photography: composition, lighting, retouching, consistency, brief fit, range.**

**For videography: storytelling, camera, sound, edit, color, pacing.**

**For content and social: platform fluency, copy, visual consistency, analytics literacy, brand safety, cadence.**

**For design: concept, craft, typography, systems thinking, file hygiene, brief fit.**

**The reviewer writes evidence, not adjectives. "Strong composition, weak flash work, brief required flash" is useful. "Great vibe" is not.**

**And here's the calibration rule: if two reviewers score the same portfolio more than twenty points apart, we review the rubric, not the candidate.**

---

## Slide 11 — Stage four: interview (37:00–40:00)

[SLIDE: Interview structure — 5 min intro, 20 min portfolio walkthrough, 10 min ways of working, 5 min questions.]

**The interview is not a vibe check. It's a structured conversation with the same shape every time.**

**Five minutes of context. Twenty minutes walking through two or three pieces: what was the brief, what did you decide, what would you do differently. Ten minutes on ways of working: feedback, deadlines, tools, conflict. Five minutes for their questions.**

**The interviewer completes a scorecard within twenty-four hours. If the scorecard is missing, the interview didn't happen. That sounds harsh. It's the only way to compare candidates fairly.**

---

## Slide 12 — Stage five: verification (40:00–43:00)

[SLIDE: Checklist — identity, tax, insurance, licensing, references, right to work.]

**Verification is boring and it is non-negotiable.**

**Identity. Tax form. Insurance where required. Licensing, including drone licenses. References. Right to work.**

**Documents go through a secure upload, never email. We record requested, received, verified, verified by, and expiry. We remind at thirty, fourteen, and seven days before expiry.**

**If a required document is missing, the contract does not get assembled. Not because we're bureaucratic, but because an uninsured shoot is an existential risk for a small agency.**

---

## Slide 13 — Stage six: the decision (43:00–46:00)

[SLIDE: One-page decision packet — Facts | Scores | Risks | Recommendation | Decision.]

**The decision packet is one page. Facts at the top. Scores in the middle. Risks and open questions. A recommendation. And a decision box.**

**The recommendation is never "hire" or "don't hire" from a machine. It's "advance," "hold for this specific item," or "decline for this specific reason."**

**A human selects. A human writes the reason. The system records who, when, and on what evidence.**

**This is the most important record we create, because it's how we get better. If we can't explain a decision six months later, we didn't make a decision. We made a mood.**

---

## Slide 14 — Stage seven: contract and onboarding (46:00–49:00)

[SLIDE: SOW → assemble → sign → training → ready.]

**Once a human approves, the system prefills the SOW and the role addendum from the profile. Rate, rate type, payment schedule, deliverables, equipment, software.**

**The human reviews the draft. Then we assemble the master agreement, the SOW, and the addendum. Signatures are tracked. We remind at forty-eight hours and seventy-two hours. At five business days, we escalate.**

**On signature, onboarding starts automatically: role lessons, integrations, a named buddy, a first-week plan. Readiness means training is one hundred percent and required integrations are verified. Not "mostly done." One hundred percent.**

---

## Slide 15 — Stage eight: staffing (49:00–52:00)

[SLIDE: Shortlist card — skill match, availability, reliability, rate fit, conflict.]

**Staffing is where the roster finally earns its keep.**

**Given a need, the system filters by role, skill, location, and eligibility. It removes anyone with an overlapping assignment. It ranks by skill match, recent relevant work, reliability, rate fit, and travel fit. It returns three to five people with a reason for each.**

**A human picks. A human confirms the rate. A human checks the conflict. Then the assignment goes out, and the talent accepts or declines.**

**No more "who's free?" No more double-booking. No more finding out on Monday that your photographer is in another state.**

---

## Slide 16 — Stage nine: delivery and QA (52:00–55:00)

[SLIDE: Lifecycle states with reviewer gates.]

**Delivery already works well in the platform. We're adding the missing discipline around it.**

**A submission must have a URL or an attachment. It routes to the right reviewer. The reviewer applies the vertical QC checklist: technical, brand, legal. A change request has a reason code and a due date. Revision count is tracked against the SOW.**

**The client accepts. Then an admin gives final approval. Not the assistant. Not the reviewer. An admin.**

**Why? Because final approval is where quality, scope, and payment meet. It deserves a named owner.**

---

## Slide 17 — Stage ten: performance and retention (55:00–57:00)

[SLIDE: Scorecard — quality, timeliness, communication, compliance, client satisfaction.]

**At project close, we record a performance scorecard: quality, timeliness, communication, compliance, and client satisfaction where we have it.**

**At quarter end, we aggregate. Who is consistently excellent? Who is improving? Who is at risk of burning out? Whose rate is out of date?**

**This is how we stop renegotiating from scratch every time and start building a real roster.**

**And when someone leaves or goes on the bench, we run an offboarding checklist: close work, revoke access, archive assets, record status, send an exit note. No one should disappear from our system without a record.**

---

## Slide 18 — What changes on Monday (57:00–59:00)

[SLIDE: Three commitments — one front door, one queue, one decision record.]

**So what changes on Monday? Three things.**

**One: every candidate comes through one front door. No more DMs as an applicant tracking system.**

**Two: every open item lives in one queue with an owner and a clock. If it's not in the queue, it's not happening.**

**Three: every consequential decision has a record: who, what, when, why, and what next.**

**That's it. We're not adding bureaucracy. We're replacing memory with a system.**

---

## Slide 19 — Close (59:00–60:00)

[SLIDE: "Human decides. System remembers." + contact + next step.]

**I'll leave you with one sentence: human decides, system remembers.**

**If you take one action this week, open the queue and find the oldest item. Give it an owner and a date. That's the whole job, repeated every day.**

**Thank you. I'll take questions.**

## Q&A bank (15 minutes)

Use these prepared answers. Do not improvise commitments.

**Q: Can I promise a candidate a rate to keep them warm?**
**A:** No. Record their expectation and tell them the team will confirm before any commitment. If they have a deadline, flag it to the Principal today.

**Q: What if a candidate is perfect but the portfolio link is private?**
**A:** Ask for an accessible link or three to five files. Pause the clock. Do not score what you cannot see.

**Q: What if the reviewer disagrees with the rubric score?**
**A:** The reviewer owns the final judgment, but they must record the override and the reason. If two reviewers are more than twenty points apart, we calibrate the rubric.

**Q: Can the AI send the rejection email?**
**A:** It can draft it. A human approves and sends it. Every decline has an objective reason and a retention option.

**Q: What counts as "verified" for a document?**
**A:** Legible, name-matched, unexpired, and covering the required scope, recorded with who verified it and when. If it's altered or unclear, stop and escalate.

**Q: What if the client wants someone who isn't available?**
**A:** Offer the closest available shortlist with the trade-offs stated. Do not promise an unavailable person. If the client insists, escalate to the Principal.

**Q: How fast do we need to respond to a new application?**
**A:** Acknowledgment within one business day. First human review within two. Decision within seven business days of a complete application.

**Q: What if we're too busy to run the process?**
**A:** That's exactly when the process matters. Run the morning queue for fifteen minutes. The system is designed for busy days, not quiet ones.

**Q: Where do I record a decision?**
**A:** In the decision record attached to the applicant, with the reason code and evidence links. If the system is unavailable, use the approved fallback log and backfill within one business day.

**Q: Who can see tax forms and IDs?**
**A:** Only the Principal and the designated ops owner, through restricted access. Never in chat, email, or an AI prompt.

## Appendix B — Demand-side insert script (optional, +6 minutes)

Cut the second half of the Q&A bank and Slide 17 detail to keep the session at 60 minutes.

**Slide D1 — Where projects come from (2 minutes)**

> **Not every inquiry is a project. Some are a person with an idea, no buyer, and no budget. If we send that to a photographer, we have wasted the photographer's day and spent our own credibility.**
>
> **So we score the listing before we touch talent: who is the buyer, can they approve spend, is there a budget, is the scope specific, are the rights defined, can we staff it, is the margin real, is the risk acceptable, and do we have a relationship.**
>
> **Four zeros cap it at "unverified" no matter how good the rest looks: identity, authority, budget, risk. A beautiful brief from an anonymous buyer is not a project.**

**Slide D2 — Requirements before matching (2 minutes)**

> **Once it is real, we extract the requirement. Role, skills, dates, location, crew size, deliverables, rights, budget band, payment terms, revisions, confidentiality.**
>
> **Every field is confirmed, inferred, or missing. Confirmed needs a quote. Missing blocks matching. We do not invent a deliverable count or a date to look responsive.**
>
> **Then, and only then, do we match.**

**Slide D3 — The shortlist and the human gate (2 minutes)**

> **Eligibility first: qualification, consent, skill, dates, location, compliance, rate, conflict, rights, confidence. A person who cannot do the work is never ranked.**
>
> **Then we rank the eligible people on skill, relevant experience, quality, reliability, availability, commercial fit, and relationship — and we explain every entry with evidence.**
>
> **The system recommends. A human selects, approves the rate, makes the offer, and confirms the start date. No automation contacts talent about an assignment. Ever.**

**Transition back:** "That's the demand side. The same rule applies on both sides: the system remembers, the human decides."
